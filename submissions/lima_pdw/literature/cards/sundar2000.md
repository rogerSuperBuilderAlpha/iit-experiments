## What it argues

Sundar and Nass ask **who the user thinks is talking**, contrasting the Computer-as-Medium model (people orient to an unseen programmer behind the machine) against the Computer-as-Source model (people orient to the machine itself). Two studies, not one four-way design. **Study 1** (*N* = 30 undergraduates, mixed 2×2: Computer-vs-Programmer label between-subjects, Praise-vs-Criticism round within-subjects): participants underwent two rounds of tutoring, testing and evaluation at a terminal labeled either "Computer #1/#2" or "Programmer #1/#2," with all references to *computer* replaced by *programmer* in that condition and the self-referential pronoun switching from "this computer" to "I." A MANOVA found a significant main effect of the label on four combined indices (*F* = 41.33, *p* < .001); univariate ANOVAs showed the Computer label rated significantly friendlier (*M* = 3.43 vs. 2.35, *F*(1,28) = 9.43, *p* < .01), more playful (*F*(1,28) = 7.0, *p* < .05), more effective (*F*(1,28) = 4.76, *p* < .05), and more stylistically similar to the participant (*F*(1,28) = 6.78, *p* < .05) than the identical content under the Programmer label. **Study 2** (*N* = 22, between-subjects only) replaced "programmer" with "networker" — operationalized as a live human tutor reached through the network, in another room, confirmed present by a staged phone call — to raise ecological validity; a MANOVA again found a significant effect (*F* = 6.9, *p* < .05), with the Computer label rated friendlier (*t*(20) = 3.47, *p* < .01) and more playful (*t*(20) = 3.46, *p* < .01) than the Networker/Tutor label, effectiveness only marginal (*p* < .10), style similarity not significant, and — the one reversal — participants reported significantly *greater excitement* with the Networker/Tutor than with the Computer (*t*(20) = 2.64, *p* < .05).

The finding is that source orientation is a variable the user supplies, not a fact the interface determines — and it is not simply "more human attribution, more positive response": the excitement reversal in Study 2 shows the two source-labels producing different profiles, not a uniform preference.

## Relation to the argument

**The single most transferable finding in this cluster for §9's third position.** Sundar and Nass show that a user coordinating through a machine is always making an implicit attribution about who is behind the message, and that the attribution has consequences. The arrangement in §7 is exactly a situation of contested source orientation: when a rating arrives, the worker must decide whether she is being told something by a rider, by a platform, by an algorithm, or by nobody — and §7's asymmetric interpretation is partly the work of settling that question.

This gives the paper something better than a structural assertion. **The triad is not just a description of the arrangement; it is a problem the worker has to solve.** Which of the three positions issued this outcome, and on whose behalf, is undetermined from the worker's side, and answering it is competence. That formulation makes the triad do explanatory work in §7 rather than sitting in §9 as a taxonomic complaint, and Sundar and Nass supply the experimental warrant.

Their machine-heuristic work (Sundar & Kim, 2019, doi:10.1145/3290605.3300768) extends the argument to trust in automated systems.

## Caution

Laboratory study, 2000-era web interfaces, student participants (Study 1 *N* = 30, Study 2 *N* = 22 — small by current standards), and Study 1's manipulation is a stated cover story rather than an ambiguity the participant resolves for herself; Study 2 improves on this by staging a live-tutor illusion, and debriefing confirmed nobody doubted it. The Computers Are Social Actors programme it belongs to has faced replication questions. Effect sizes above are now verified and citable (ω² ranges .11–.34 in Study 1); the Study 2 excitement reversal is a genuine complication worth citing, not just the headline friendliness/playfulness effect.
