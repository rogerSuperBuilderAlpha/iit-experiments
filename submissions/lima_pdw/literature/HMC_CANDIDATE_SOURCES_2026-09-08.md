# HMC hearing — candidate sources to deepen the hearing, 2026-09-08

Research proposal only. Nothing here has been added to `manuscript/PAPER.md`. Scope: find literature in
the Human–Machine Communication tradition that either sharpens the hearing's boundary claim (a near-miss
that still falls short of a triadic, binding intermediary) or threatens it (something that gets closer
than the paper currently admits). Checked against `literature/cards/` (409 entries) and PAPER.md's
current citation list first — none of the five below is already cited or carded.

Verdict up front: **the boundary claim survives.** Nothing found occupies the position (an intermediary
that independently evaluates and binds two human parties in interdependent work). The closest new
candidate (Daryanto et al. 2026) is explicitly facilitative and says so; the second-closest (Muniz
& Santos 2026) argues at the institutional/relational level, not the individual-capacity level the
construct claims. Both are worth citing precisely because they are recent, genuine attempts that still
fall short — a stronger "I looked and it isn't there" than the hearing currently has.

## 1. Daryanto, T., Ding, X., Ping, K., Wilhelm, L. T., Chen, Y., Brown, C., & Rho, E. H. (2026). Human-Human-AI Triadic Programming: Uncovering the Role of AI Agent and the Value of Human Partner in Collaborative Learning. In *Proceedings of the 2026 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems* (pp. 1–21). ACM. https://doi.org/10.1145/3772318.3791773 — Crossref-verified 2026-09-08.

**Verified:** fetched in substantial part via arXiv:2601.12134v1 (the CHI camera-ready). Within-subjects
study, 20 CS students (10 pre-existing pairs), three conditions (shared AI / personal AI / human-AI
baseline), three LeetCode-adapted tasks, 90-minute session. The AI agent gives hints and code analysis
and explicit "lightweight proactive interventions" designed to avoid "premature solution reveal" —
**no binding determination; participants can accept or ignore every suggestion.** One anomalous pair
(P15+P16, 56% AI-generated code despite the triadic condition) shows the paper noticing that weak
human-to-human communication undermines the arrangement's accountability effect — but the paper does
not measure that capacity, it only observes its absence in one pair.

**What it would add.** This is the closest thing yet found to the paper's own structure — literally
"triadic," two humans plus one AI, published 2026, at CHI — and it falls on the facilitative side of the
line explicitly, in the paper's own words ("understand before applying," not enforce). It is a stronger,
more contemporary instance of the move the hearing already makes with Seeber et al. and Sebo et al.
(machines among groups, proposing/facilitating, never binding) and could either replace or sit beside
Seeber et al.'s agenda-setting paragraph — the 8 Sept panel already flagged Seeber's paragraph as the
one most safely cut if HMC needs to shrink; this could be the stronger citation in its place, since it
is data-bearing, not agenda-setting. It also supplies a small but real result — the anomalous pair — that
could sharpen the paper's own "coordination capacity, unmeasured elsewhere" argument with a fresh,
2026, on-point example.

**Handling: safe strengthening addition.** Falls unambiguously short of the paper's boundary; no caution
needed on the gap claim itself.

## 2. Muniz, V., & Santos, É. (2026). Between humans and algorithms: Framing machine communication as a socio-technical relation. *AI & Society, 41*(6), 5573–5587. https://doi.org/10.1007/s00146-026-03041-6

**Correction, Crossref-verified 2026-09-08:** the family name is **Muniz**, not "Vander Muniz" — Crossref's structured metadata gives given name "Vander," family name "Muniz." Cite as Muniz (2026) or Muniz & Santos (2026), not "Vander Muniz." Second author's given name is Ébida.

**Verified: abstract only**, via Crossref metadata (the full text sits behind a Springer login gate that
blocked every route tried). *Do not cite beyond what the abstract supports until a fuller read.*
Theoretical essay proposing an integrative framework for "machine speech" — computationally generated
content that "gains institutional authority and shapes decisions in social contexts" — via three
modalities (complementarity, substitution, coconstruction), drawing on Wiener, McLuhan, and Minsky. A
search-engine summary of the piece (not the primary text) surfaced its own worked example: a credit
score "communicates because the institution treats its output as a legitimate judgment that coordinates
the actions of loan officers, applicants, and regulators" — binding language close to the paper's own
definition, but describing a multi-party institutional arrangement, not an individual's coordination
capacity within it.

**What it would add, if the full text confirms the abstract's framing.** This is a 2026 theoretical
piece using strikingly similar vocabulary to the paper's own ("institutional authority," "binds and
informs what other actors can do next" per the search summary) while — on the evidence available —
staying at the level of distributed institutional agency rather than asking what an individual needs to
be able to do. If that reading holds, it is the sharpest available instance of the move the hearing
already makes with Curchod et al. and Cameron in the synthesis section: the field is now producing
theory that describes the arrangement's binding structure but still does not ask the paper's question.
That would make it a genuinely valuable citation — proof the position is visible to the field and still
unclaimed.

**Handling: caution — full text required before citing.** The credit-score example was read via a
search-engine summary of the article, not the primary text; a mischaracterization here would be exactly
the kind of thing a hostile reviewer catches, since the citation would be doing real argumentative work.
Springer institutional access needed.

## 3. Wenker, K. (2023). Who wrote this? How smart replies impact language and agency in the workplace. *Telematics and Informatics Reports, 10*, Article 100062. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.teler.2023.100062 — Crossref-verified 2026-09-08 (published version; supersedes the arXiv/SSRN preprint used for the read).

**Verified:** fetched via arXiv abstract page. Mixed-methods, crowdsourced experiment testing a "loss of
agency theory" — smart-reply adoption shifts perceived authorship of one's own message, driven by time
pressure, ease-seeking, and priming; the paper's own framing is explicitly non-zero-sum (human and
machine agency "enhance, displace, and reinforce" each other rather than trading off). Dyadic in
structure: a single sender's relationship to her own message, not an evaluation of two parties.

**What it would add.** A workplace-specific extension of the Hancock et al./Hohenstein & Jung lineage
already in the hearing — directly on point for a paper explicitly about coordination *at work*, which
none of the currently-cited AI-MC sources are (Hohenstein & Jung and Hohenstein et al. are both
social/interpersonal chat contexts). Would sit naturally beside the Hohenstein sentences in the
AI-Mediated Communication subsection as the workplace instance of the same mechanism.

**Handling: safe strengthening addition**, though note the venue (*Telematics and Informatics Reports*)
is a lower-tier journal than the hearing's other AI-MC sources (Hancock et al. in *JCMC*, Hohenstein in
*Computers in Human Behavior* and *Scientific Reports*) — cite for the workplace framing, not as a peer
of those three.

## 4. Khadpe, P., Wenzel, K., Loewenstein, G., & Kaufman, G. (2025). Explaining the reputational risks of AI-mediated communication: Messages labeled as AI-assisted are viewed as less diagnostic of the sender's moral character. *Proceedings of the AAAI/ACM Conference on AI, Ethics, and Society, 8*, 1413–1423. https://doi.org/10.1609/aies.v8i2.36641 — Crossref-verified 2026-09-08 (published version).

**Verified:** fetched via arXiv abstract. Two vignette studies, 399 participants total, varying whether a
message (warmth-signaling, e.g. an apology, or coldness-signaling, e.g. assigning blame) carried an
AI-assistance label. Finding: the label does not make senders look worse outright; it makes the message
less *diagnostic* of character either way — a warm message reads as less warm, a cold message as less
cold, when AI-labeled ("signal diagnosticity"). Dyadic: sender-message-perceiver, no evaluation binding
two parties.

**What it would add.** A more recent (2025, AIES — a rigorous, ethics-focused HCI/AI venue) and more
theoretically developed account of the same suspicion/attribution family already in the hearing
(Hohenstein & Jung's crumple zone, Hohenstein et al.'s perceived-use penalty). The "signal diagnosticity"
mechanism is a genuine addition — it explains *why* AI-mediated messages read differently, not just that
they do — and would strengthen the paragraph currently ending on Hohenstein et al. without displacing it;
best used as a one-clause mechanism gloss rather than a new paragraph.

**Handling: safe strengthening addition.**

## 5. Zhu, J., & Molnar, A. (2026). Blissful (A)Ignorance: Despite the widespread adoption of AI in communication, people do not suspect AI use in realistic contexts. *Computers in Human Behavior, 180*, Article 108929. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2026.108929 — Crossref-verified 2026-09-08.

Already surfaced and verified by the earlier HMC audit this same day
(`HMC_EXPERIMENTAL_DESIGNS_2026-09-08.md` §3) — repeated here only because it belongs on this shortlist
too, and to avoid the author having to cross-reference two files. Two online experiments, >1,300 U.S.
adults, naturalistic communication contexts. Confirms the suspicion penalty fires when triggered but
shows it rarely triggers outside the lab — the strongest available qualification of PAPER.md line 77.

**Handling: safe strengthening addition**, already recommended in the prior file.

## What did not make the list

Searches for a genuinely triadic, *binding* structure inside the HMC/communication tradition
specifically (as opposed to organization theory, where Curchod et al. and Cameron already cover this)
turned up nothing that clears the bar — the field's closest instances (Daryanto et al., Muniz &
Santos) both fall short in ways worth citing rather than worth worrying about. No replication or
extension of Sundar & Nass (2000) or Jung, Martelaro & Hinds (2015) was found. No paper was found that
threatens the boundary claim outright.
